§1
Why Data Collection Matters More Than Your Model

Here's something they don't teach you enough in courses: a mediocre model on good data beats a great model on bad data every single time. I've seen teams spend weeks tuning XGBoost hyperparameters while their training data had a collection bias that made the entire thing useless.

Think about it simply — if you want to know what food Indians prefer, and you only survey people in Mumbai's 5-star hotels, your data is wrong before you even start analyzing. Your model will be confidently, precisely wrong. That's worse than no model at all.

Data Collection = the process of gathering raw information from the real world. Sampling = deciding which part of that world you actually measure — because you almost never have the time or money to measure everything. Get either of these wrong and all your analysis is built on a lie.

❌ Real Mistake I've Seen

A startup built a customer satisfaction model. Survey was sent only via email. Result: only tech-savvy, younger, urban customers responded. Older customers — who were actually churning — were completely missed. Model said "customers are 78% happy." Reality: churn was at an all-time high. Collection bias killed the entire project.

✅ The Right Way

Same survey — sent via email, SMS, IVR phone call, and in-store kiosk. Multiple collection channels ensured all customer segments responded. Survey results were representative. Model accurately predicted which customers were at risk. Churn reduced by 22% in 3 months.

§2
Primary vs Secondary Data

Every dataset you'll ever work with came from one of two places — either someone collected it fresh specifically for this problem (primary), or someone else collected it before for a different reason and you're reusing it (secondary). Both are valid, but they come with very different trade-offs.

Source Type A

🎯 Primary Data

You go out and collect it yourself, fresh, for your specific problem. It's tailored exactly to your question. Expensive and slow, but highly relevant and reliable.

How it's collected:
Surveys & Questionnaires Interviews Experiments / A/B tests Observations Focus groups Sensor / IoT data
Real Example
Swiggy wants to know why users abandon the cart. They run an in-app survey that pops up right after cart abandonment. That's primary — collected fresh, exactly for this question. Very relevant. Cost: dev time + user experience interruption.
✅ Pros
Exact fit for your question. You control quality. Fresh and current.
❌ Cons
Expensive. Time-consuming. Needs careful design to avoid bias.
Source Type B

📦 Secondary Data

Someone else collected it for a different purpose. You're reusing it. Faster and cheaper to get — but you have less control over how it was collected and whether it truly fits your problem.

Where it comes from:
Government databases (census, RBI) Research papers Company's own historical data Kaggle / Public datasets Third-party data providers
Real Example
HDFC Bank wants to build a credit risk model. They use RBI's published borrower data + their own 10 years of historical loan records. Both are secondary — collected earlier for other purposes (regulatory reporting), but now reused for ML. Saves 2+ years of data collection.
✅ Pros
Fast and cheap. Often large volumes. Historical data available.
❌ Cons
May not fit your exact question. Quality unknown. Could be outdated.
💡 In real DS projects, you almost always use both. Secondary data gives you the historical baseline and scale. Primary data fills the gaps where secondary data doesn't have what you need. Example: use past transaction data (secondary) + run a fresh customer survey (primary) for a churn model.
§3
What is Sampling? The Core Idea

Imagine you're a doctor checking if someone has a fever. You don't boil their entire body — you put a thermometer under their arm for 30 seconds. That small measurement tells you about the whole body's condition. That is exactly what sampling is.

In data science: your population is everyone/everything you care about (all 1.4 billion Indians, all 5 million app users). Your sample is the smaller group you actually measure. If your sample is chosen well — it represents the population accurately. Choose it badly — and your conclusions are wrong for everyone.

🩺

The Doctor Analogy: A blood test takes 5ml of blood to understand what's happening in your entire 5 litres of blood (0.1% sample). It works because blood is well-mixed and the sample is taken correctly. If the doctor only took blood from a bruised area — the result would be wrong. Same principle in DS: method of sampling determines if results are trustworthy.

4 Sampling Methods — When to Use Which

There is no single "best" sampling method. Each one is the right tool for a specific situation. A good data scientist knows all four and chooses based on the problem. Let's break each one down with a concrete, easy-to-remember example.

01

Simple Random Sampling

🎰 "Lucky Draw" method

Every single person/item in the population has an equal chance of being selected. Completely random — like pulling names from a hat. No rules, no structure, just pure randomness.

Real Project Example
Flipkart QA Testing: 50,000 orders shipped today. We want to manually quality-check 500 of them. We assign every order a number and use a random number generator to pick 500. No pattern, pure random. Each order had equal chance of being picked.
✅ Best when
Population is similar (homogeneous) and you have a complete list of everyone.

02

📏 "Every Nth person" method

Pick a random starting point, then select every Nth item from a list. Structured and easy to execute in the field without needing a computer. Very practical for large, ordered populations.

Real Project Example
Bank ATM Survey: We want to survey ATM users about their experience. Instead of picking randomly (messy to do in person), we instruct the field agent: "Survey every 5th person who walks out of the ATM." Simple, repeatable, no laptop needed. Works beautifully in the field.
✅ Best when
Population is ordered (queue, assembly line, list). Easy to implement without tech.
❌ Watch out
If there's a hidden pattern at every Nth position (e.g., shift changes at same interval), you'll get biased results.

Quick Comparison — Which Method to Use When?
Method How it works Best for Common DS Use Case Complexity
Simple Random Every item has equal chance — pure random draw Similar / homogeneous populations QA testing, A/B test assignment Low
Systematic Random start → every Nth item selected Ordered lists, field surveys Factory QC, ATM user surveys Low
Stratified Split into subgroups → random sample from each Diverse populations with distinct subgroups Customer segmentation, elections Medium
Cluster Split into clusters → pick a few → survey all in them Geographically spread populations Rural surveys, retail store sampling High
§5
Sample Size — How Many is Enough?

This is the most common question I get from junior data scientists: "How many samples do I need?" Too few samples and your results are unreliable (too much noise). Too many and you're wasting money and time collecting data you don't need. There's a formula — but more importantly, there's an intuition behind it.

The key insight: Sample size depends on how much error you're willing to accept and how confident you want to be. If you're testing a new drug (life or death), you need near-perfect accuracy → huge sample. If you're testing whether a blue button vs orange button gets more clicks → much smaller sample is fine.

n = (Z² × p × (1 − p)) / E²
n

Sample size you need to collect

Z

Confidence level score
95% confidence → Z = 1.96
99% confidence → Z = 2.58

p

Expected proportion
Use 0.5 if unknown (gives max sample size — safest choice)

E

Margin of error you can accept
5% margin → E = 0.05
Smaller E = bigger sample needed

Simple example: Zomato wants to survey customer satisfaction. They want to be 95% confident, with a 5% margin of error, and don't know the expected proportion → use p = 0.5.

n = (1.96² × 0.5 × 0.5) / 0.05² = (3.84 × 0.25) / 0.0025 = 0.96 / 0.0025 = 384 people

Zomato has 80 million users. But they only need to survey 384 people to get results that are 95% accurate within ±5%. That's the power of proper sampling — 384 out of 80 million (0.0005%) is enough!

⚡ Practical rule of thumb from experience: For most business problems — 385 samples gives 95% confidence at 5% error. For life-critical work (medicine, safety) — aim for 99% confidence (2.58 Z-score). For quick internal experiments — even 100-200 samples can give useful directional insights. Always calculate before collecting, not after.
§6
Data Quality Checks

Once you have your data — don't start analyzing immediately. Run quality checks first. In my experience, raw data from the real world almost always has at least 2–3 quality issues. Finding them before analysis saves you from building a model on garbage. Finding them after — when you've already presented to stakeholders — is a career-damaging embarrassment I've seen happen.

🕳️

Missing Values

Empty cells in your data. Could be because someone skipped a survey question, a sensor malfunctioned, or a system glitch. Must be handled before any model.

Age column: 1,000 rows, but 87 rows have blank age. That's 8.7% missing. Ignoring this crashes most ML algorithms.
Fix: Impute with median / flag as separate category / drop if <5%
👽

Outliers

Values that are extreme — way too high or too low compared to the rest. Sometimes real (a billionaire in a salary survey). Sometimes errors (age = 999).

Salary data: most values ₹3L–₹20L. One row says ₹99,99,999. Data entry error or Mukesh Ambani? Must investigate.
Fix: Box plot / IQR method / cap at 99th percentile / delete if error
👯

Duplicate Records

The same row appearing multiple times. Happens from system bugs, double form submissions, or merging two databases that had overlapping records.

Customer database: Ramesh Gupta appears 3 times with same phone number. All 3 rows are identical — system logged the signup 3 times.
Fix: df.drop_duplicates() in pandas / check on unique ID columns
🏷️

Inconsistent Labels

Same thing written in different ways across rows. Makes your model think they're different categories when they're actually the same thing.

City column: "mumbai", "Mumbai", "MUMBAI", "Bombay", "mum" — all mean the same city but appear as 5 different values to a machine.
Fix: .str.lower().strip() + mapping dictionary to standardize
⏰

Wrong Data Types

A number stored as text, a date stored as a plain string. Pandas will read "₹45,000" as an object (text), not a number. You can't do math on it until it's fixed.

Revenue column imported from CSV: "45,000.00" — looks like a number, but the comma makes Python treat it as a string. df.mean() returns an error.
Fix: pd.to_numeric() / pd.to_datetime() / regex to remove commas
⚖️

Sampling Bias

Your sample doesn't represent the actual population. The most dangerous quality issue because it's hardest to detect — your data looks fine but conclusions are systematically wrong.

Surveying app users only via push notification — people who've turned off notifications (often less engaged users) are automatically excluded. Your "satisfaction score" is biased high.
Fix: Compare sample demographics vs known population demographics
§7
Survey Design Principles

When you're collecting primary data through a survey — the way you write your questions determines the quality of the data you get back. A badly designed survey gives you confidently wrong data. I've reviewed hundreds of surveys in my career and the same mistakes come up again and again. Here are the principles that separate good surveys from bad ones.

01

Ask One Thing at a Time

Never put two questions in one. The respondent doesn't know which part to answer, and you can't interpret the result.

❌ "Was the delivery fast and the packaging good?"
✅ Q1: "Was the delivery fast?" Q2: "Was the packaging good?"
02

Use Neutral Language

Loaded or leading words push respondents toward a particular answer — your data reflects your bias, not their opinion.

❌ "Don't you agree that our amazing app saves you time?"
✅ "How much time does using this app save you per day?"
03

Offer Complete Options

If your answer choices don't cover all possibilities, respondents are forced to lie. Always include "Other" or "Not applicable".

❌ How do you pay? [Cash / Card] — what about UPI?
✅ [Cash / Card / UPI / Net Banking / Other]
04

Keep It Short

Every extra question reduces completion rate. More than 10 questions and you start losing respondents — especially on mobile. Quality over quantity.

❌ 45-question survey → 12% completion rate
✅ 7-question survey → 68% completion rate + better quality answers
05

Pilot Test First

Send the survey to 10–20 people internally before launching. You'll catch confusing questions, missing options, and technical bugs before they corrupt your data at scale.

❌ Launch to 10,000 users → discover "Submit" button was broken on iOS after 3 days
✅ 15-person pilot → caught the iOS bug → fixed → clean data collected
06

Avoid Recall Bias

Asking people to remember the past is unreliable. The longer ago, the less accurate. Always ask about recent experiences where possible.

❌ "How many times did you order food online in the last year?"
✅ "How many times did you order food online in the last 7 days?"
§8
Real Project — Sampling Everything Together
⚡ Real Project · Customer Satisfaction Study · FMCG / Consumer Goods

Hindustan Unilever Style — Pan-India Customer Satisfaction Survey for a New Soap Brand

The brand had just launched in 8 states. 3 months post-launch, leadership wanted to know: are customers satisfied? Would they repurchase? What's working vs not? Classic primary data collection + sampling problem.

Step 1 · Data Source Decision
Chose primary data — no secondary data exists for a new product
Method: in-store intercept survey (field agents approach shoppers) + post-purchase SMS survey sent via retailer POS system
Two channels = reduced single-channel bias (not just digital-savvy users)
Step 2 · Sampling Method
Used Stratified Sampling — divided India into 4 zones (North/South/East/West) and 3 city tiers (Metro/Tier-2/Rural)
12 strata total — proportional sample from each to match actual sales distribution
Ensured rural voice wasn't drowned out by urban respondents (common mistake)
Step 3 · Sample Size Calc
Needed 95% confidence, 5% margin of error → formula gave n = 384 per stratum
12 strata × 384 = 4,608 total surveys needed
Added 20% buffer for non-responses → targeted 5,530 surveys
Step 4 · Quality Checks Run
Found 340 duplicate entries (same phone number submitted twice) — removed
City field had 14 variations of "Bangalore/Bengaluru/blr" — standardized
Rating column had 3 entries with value "0" — invalid (scale was 1–5) — treated as missing
Pilot with 50 people caught that Q4 was confusing — reworded before full launch
💡 Final Result: 5,211 clean responses collected. Satisfaction score: 3.8/5 overall, but only 2.9/5 in rural South India. Without stratified sampling, the rural South signal would have been lost in the aggregate score. Brand used this to reformulate the scent specifically for South Indian preferences. Next quarter: repurchase rate in that region jumped 34%.
Chapter Summary

What you must take away from this chapter ↓

Garbage in, garbage out. No model fixes bad data. Data collection quality determines your entire project's reliability — not the algorithm you choose.

Primary = fresh, yours, expensive. Secondary = existing, fast, cheap. Real projects use both — secondary for scale/history, primary to fill gaps.

Simple Random = equal chance for all. Systematic = every Nth person. Stratified = divide into groups, sample from each. Cluster = pick whole groups.

384 people = 95% confidence at 5% error — regardless of whether your population is 10,000 or 80 million. This number surprises everyone the first time they see it.

Always run quality checks first: missing values, outliers, duplicates, inconsistent labels, wrong data types, sampling bias. Find them before analysis, not after.

Survey design = data quality. One question at a time, neutral language, complete options, short surveys, pilot test first. Bad questions = useless data, no matter how large the sample.
