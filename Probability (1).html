§ 08

Introduction to Probability

📌 8.1 — What is Probability?

Probability is the measure of how likely an event is to occur. It is a number between 0 and 1 (or 0% to 100%) that tells us how certain or uncertain something is.

Think of it as a scale of belief — from "definitely won't happen" (0) to "definitely will happen" (1). Everything uncertain lives somewhere in between.

Core Formula
P(A) = (Number of favorable outcomes) / (Total number of possible outcomes)

P(A) is always:   0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1
Probability Scale — 0 to 1
📐 Worked Example — Coin Toss

When you flip a fair coin, there are 2 equally likely outcomes: Heads or Tails.

1

Total outcomes = {Heads, Tails} → 2

2

Favorable outcomes for "Heads" = {Heads} → 1

3

P(Heads) = 1/2 = 0.5 = 50%

4

P(Tails) = 1/2 = 0.5 → Both together = 1 (certainty that some outcome occurs)

📊 Data Science Applications
  • Model Confidence: Every ML classifier outputs a probability (e.g., "This email is spam with P = 0.93"). Decision boundary is usually set at 0.5.
  • Risk Scoring: Credit models assign P(default) to each loan applicant. Banks use this to approve/reject loans.
  • A/B Testing: Statistical significance is expressed as probability. "p-value = 0.03" means only 3% chance results are due to randomness.
  • Reinforcement Learning: Agents choose actions based on probability distributions over possible rewards.

🎯 8.2 — Sample Space and Events

Before calculating any probability, we must define the universe of all possibilities.

Sample Space (S or Ω)

The complete set of ALL possible outcomes of an experiment. It is the universe of everything that can happen. Denoted as S or Ω (Omega).

Die roll: S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}

Event (A, B, C…)

An event is any subset of the sample space. It is the specific outcome or collection of outcomes we are interested in. Can be one outcome or many.

Even number: A = {2, 4, 6}

📐 Worked Example — Rolling Two Dice

Experiment: Roll 2 dice and find P(sum = 7)

S = {(1,1),(1,2),...,(6,6)} → Total outcomes = 6 × 6 = 36
1

Event A = sum of 7: {(1,6),(2,5),(3,4),(4,3),(5,2),(6,1)} → 6 outcomes

2

P(sum=7) = 6/36 = 1/6 ≈ 0.167

3

7 is the most probable sum when rolling two dice — this is why 7 is the most common result in board games like Monopoly.

Visual — Sum Frequencies for Two Dice (Total Outcomes = 36)

🔬 8.3 — Types of Probability

There are three approaches to assigning probability values, each used in different situations:

1. Classical Probability

Based on logical reasoning and symmetry. Assumes all outcomes are equally likely. No experiment needed — just counting.

P(A) = Favorable / Total

Example: P(King from deck) = 4/52 = 1/13

2. Empirical Probability

Based on observed data / experiments. You actually run the experiment many times and count frequencies. More realistic for real-world data.

P(A) = Freq of A / Total trials

Example: Out of 1000 emails, 230 were spam → P(spam) = 0.23

3. Subjective Probability

Based on personal belief, expert judgment, or intuition. No formula. Used when data is scarce or events are unique. Basis of Bayesian statistics.

P(A) = Expert's belief (0–1)

Example: A doctor estimates 70% chance a patient has a disease based on symptoms alone.

📌

In Data Science: Empirical probability is used for model training (learning from data). Subjective probability is the foundation of Bayesian ML models — your prior belief gets updated as more data arrives.

§ 09

Probability Rules

⟂ 9.1 — Complement Rule

The complement of event A (written as A' or Aᶜ) is the event that A does NOT happen. Together, A and its complement cover the entire sample space — their probabilities must add up to 1.

Complement Rule
P(Aᶜ) = 1 − P(A)
Or equivalently:   P(A) + P(Aᶜ) = 1
📐 Worked Example

In a dataset of 500 patients, 120 tested positive for diabetes.

1

P(Positive) = 120/500 = 0.24

2

P(Not Positive) = 1 − 0.24 = 0.76

3

Verify: 0.24 + 0.76 = 1.0 ✓

💡

DS Use: "At least one" probability problems are always easier with complement: P(at least one fraud) = 1 − P(no fraud at all). Used constantly in hypothesis testing, ROC curve analysis, and error rate calculations.

➕ 9.2 — Addition Rule (OR)

The addition rule finds the probability that at least one of two events occurs (event A OR event B).

General Addition Rule (any events)
P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)

For Mutually Exclusive events (A and B cannot both happen):
P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B)    [because P(A ∩ B) = 0]
Venn Diagram — Addition Rule
📐 Worked Example — Card Draw

From a standard 52-card deck: P(drawing a Heart OR a King)?

1

P(Heart) = 13/52 = 0.25

2

P(King) = 4/52 = 0.077

3

P(Heart AND King) = King of Hearts = 1/52 = 0.019

4

P(Heart OR King) = 0.25 + 0.077 − 0.019 = 0.308 ≈ 30.8%

5

Why subtract? The King of Hearts was counted TWICE — once in Hearts, once in Kings. So we remove one overlap.

✖️ 9.3 — Multiplication Rule (AND)

The multiplication rule finds the probability that two events both occur (event A AND event B).

General Multiplication Rule (dependent events)
P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B | A)

For Independent events (A does not affect B):
P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B)
📐 Worked Example — Drawing Cards Without Replacement

P(drawing 2 Aces in a row) from a 52-card deck, without replacement:

1

P(1st Ace) = 4/52 = 1/13

2

After removing 1 Ace: 51 cards remain, 3 Aces remain.

3

P(2nd Ace | 1st was Ace) = 3/51 = 1/17

4

P(both Aces) = 1/13 × 1/17 = 1/221 ≈ 0.0045 (0.45%)

5

These are dependent events — drawing the first Ace changes the probability of the second.

🔗 9.4 — Conditional Probability P(A|B)

Conditional probability asks: Given that B has already happened, what is the probability of A? The vertical bar "|" means "given that". It narrows our sample space from the entire S to just the event B.

Conditional Probability Formula
P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)    [provided P(B) > 0]

Read as: "Probability of A given B has occurred"
📐 Worked Example — Medical Diagnosis

Data: 1000 patients tested for a disease.

Sick + Test Positive: 90 | Sick + Test Negative: 10
Healthy + Test Positive: 50 | Healthy + Test Negative: 850
1

P(Sick) = 100/1000 = 0.10

2

P(Test+) = (90+50)/1000 = 140/1000 = 0.14

3

P(Sick AND Test+) = 90/1000 = 0.09

4

P(Sick | Test+) = 0.09 / 0.14 = 0.643 (64.3%)

5

Meaning: If a patient tests positive, there is a 64.3% chance they actually have the disease. The other 35.7% are false positives.

🤖

ML Connection: Conditional probability is the backbone of Naive Bayes classifiers, Hidden Markov Models (HMMs for speech/text), and any probabilistic model. P(class | features) is exactly what classifiers are trying to estimate.

⚖️ 9.5 — Independence vs. Dependence

Two events are independent if knowing that one occurred tells us nothing about whether the other will occur. Otherwise, they are dependent.

Test for Independence
Events A and B are INDEPENDENT if and only if:
P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B)

Equivalently: P(A | B) = P(A)   [B gives no information about A]
Aspect Independent Events Dependent Events
Definition Outcome of one doesn't affect the other Outcome of one changes probability of other
Formula P(A∩B) = P(A) × P(B) P(A∩B) = P(A) × P(B|A)
Example Flipping coin twice; rolling two dice Drawing cards without replacement
ML Use Naive Bayes assumes features are independent Bayesian Networks model dependencies between features
⚠️

Important in ML: Naive Bayes classifier assumes all features are independent given the class label — which is rarely true in practice. Yet despite this "naive" assumption, it works remarkably well for text classification and spam detection. This is one of the most interesting paradoxes in applied ML.

§ 10

Counting Methods

! 10.1 — Factorial

Before permutations and combinations, we need factorials. The factorial of n (written n!) is the product of all positive integers from 1 to n. It counts the number of ways to arrange n distinct items.

Factorial Formula
n! = n × (n−1) × (n−2) × ... × 2 × 1

Special cases:   0! = 1    1! = 1
📐 Quick Calculations
3! = 3×2×1 = 6
5! = 5×4×3×2×1 = 120
10! = 3,628,800
0! = 1 (by definition — one way to arrange nothing)

Intuition: How many ways can 3 people (Alice, Bob, Carol) sit in a row? 3! = 6 arrangements: ABC, ACB, BAC, BCA, CAB, CBA.

🔢 10.2 — Permutations (Order Matters)

A permutation counts the number of ways to select AND arrange r items from n items where order matters. Changing the order gives a different permutation.

Use permutations when: position matters (1st place vs 2nd place), order changes meaning (password "ABC" ≠ "BAC"), or you're arranging things in a sequence.

Permutation Formula
P(n, r) = n! / (n − r)!

Where: n = total items available, r = items to arrange/select
📐 Worked Example 1 — Race Positions

In a race with 8 runners, how many ways can Gold, Silver, Bronze be awarded? (Order matters — Gold ≠ Silver)

1

n = 8 (total runners), r = 3 (positions to fill)

2

P(8, 3) = 8! / (8−3)! = 8! / 5! = (8×7×6×5!) / 5! = 8×7×6 = 336

3

There are 336 possible ways to award the medals.

📐 Worked Example 2 — 4-Digit PIN

How many unique 4-digit PINs can be formed from digits 0–9 with no repetition?

1

n = 10 (digits 0–9), r = 4 (PIN length)

2

P(10, 4) = 10!/6! = 10×9×8×7 = 5040 unique PINs

📊 Data Science Applications — Permutations
  • Password Strength: Security analysts compute permutations to estimate brute-force attack time. Longer passwords = exponentially more permutations.
  • Feature Ordering: Testing all orderings of features in sequential feature selection uses permutations.
  • Ranking Problems: Recommendation systems (Top-K lists) — how many distinct ranked lists of 5 movies exist from 1000 movies? P(1000, 5).
  • Time Series: Permutation entropy measures complexity and randomness of time series data — used in anomaly detection.

🃏 10.3 — Combinations (Order Doesn't Matter)

A combination counts the number of ways to select r items from n items where order does NOT matter. {A, B, C} is the same as {C, A, B} — it's the same group.

Use combinations when: selecting a team/group, choosing items from a set, order has no meaning, or you care about what is selected, not the sequence.

Combination Formula
C(n, r) = n! / [r! × (n − r)!]    also written as ⁿCᵣ or C(n,r)

Relationship:   C(n,r) = P(n,r) / r!   [divide by r! to remove order]
📐 Worked Example 1 — Lottery

A lottery requires picking 6 numbers from 1–49. How many combinations exist?

1

n = 49, r = 6 (order doesn't matter — {3,7,12} = {12,3,7})

2

C(49, 6) = 49! / (6! × 43!) = (49×48×47×46×45×44) / (6×5×4×3×2×1) = 13,983,816

3

About 1 in 14 million chance to win. This is why lotteries are profitable!

📐 Worked Example 2 — ML Team Selection

You have 10 features. How many ways can you choose 3 features as a subset?

1

C(10, 3) = 10! / (3! × 7!) = (10×9×8) / (3×2×1) = 720/6 = 120 subsets

2

Brute-force feature selection with 10 features means testing 120 different 3-feature combinations.

Permutation vs Combination — Key Difference
📊 Data Science Applications — Combinations
  • Feature Selection: Exhaustive search through all C(n, k) feature subsets to find the best k features. With 20 features, C(20,5) = 15,504 subsets to test.
  • Cross-Validation Splits: The number of ways to split data into train/test groups is a combination problem.
  • Ensemble Methods: Bagging in Random Forests selects C(n, k) bootstrap samples. The diversity of combinations improves generalization.
  • Hyperparameter Grid Search: Number of parameter combinations to test = product of choices. Combinatorial explosion motivates random search over grid search.
  • NLP: n-gram counting uses combinations — C(n, 2) for bigrams tells you how many word pairs to track in a vocabulary.

§ 11

Bayes' Theorem

🧠 11.1 — Understanding Bayes' Theorem

Bayes' Theorem tells us how to update our belief about a hypothesis when new evidence arrives. It combines what we already believe (prior) with what the data tells us (likelihood) to produce a refined belief (posterior).

Developed by Reverend Thomas Bayes (1763), it is arguably the most important theorem in modern machine learning and statistics.

Bayes' Theorem
P(A | B) = [ P(B | A) × P(A) ] / P(B)

Expanded denominator: P(B) = P(B|A)×P(A) + P(B|Aᶜ)×P(Aᶜ)

Posterior = [ Likelihood × Prior ] / Evidence

📐 Worked Example — Medical Test (Full Calculation)

Setup: A disease affects 1% of the population. A test for it is 95% accurate.

P(Disease) = 0.01    → Prior: 1% have disease
P(No Disease) = 0.99
P(Test+ | Disease) = 0.95    → 95% true positive rate
P(Test+ | No Disease) = 0.05   → 5% false positive rate

Question: You test positive. What is the actual probability you have the disease?

1

Calculate P(Test+) [total probability of testing positive]:
= P(Test+|Disease)×P(Disease) + P(Test+|No Disease)×P(No Disease)
= (0.95 × 0.01) + (0.05 × 0.99)
= 0.0095 + 0.0495 = 0.059

2

Apply Bayes' Theorem:
P(Disease | Test+) = P(Test+|Disease) × P(Disease) / P(Test+)
= (0.95 × 0.01) / 0.059
= 0.0095 / 0.059 = 0.161 = 16.1%

3

Interpretation: Even with a 95% accurate test, a positive result only means a 16.1% chance of actually having the disease! Because the disease is rare (1%), most positives are false alarms.

4

This is called the Base Rate Fallacy — ignoring how rare an event is. Doctors and diagnosticians must always account for base rates.

📧 11.3 — Application: Spam Detection

This is one of the most famous real-world applications of Bayes' Theorem. We want to classify an email as spam or not spam based on the words it contains.

Bayes for Spam Detection
P(Spam | "free") = P("free" | Spam) × P(Spam) / P("free")

We check for each word: given we see this word, does that raise or lower probability of spam?
📐 Step-by-Step Spam Example

Training data: 100 emails → 40 spam, 60 legitimate

Word "FREE" appears in 35 out of 40 spam emails
Word "FREE" appears in 5 out of 60 legit emails
1

Prior: P(Spam) = 40/100 = 0.40,   P(Legit) = 0.60

2

Likelihood: P("FREE" | Spam) = 35/40 = 0.875

3

Likelihood: P("FREE" | Legit) = 5/60 = 0.083

4

P("FREE") = (0.875×0.40) + (0.083×0.60) = 0.35 + 0.05 = 0.40

5

Posterior: P(Spam | "FREE") = (0.875 × 0.40) / 0.40 = 0.875 = 87.5%

6

An email containing "FREE" has an 87.5% chance of being spam. The filter marks it accordingly.

🤖 11.4 — Naive Bayes Classifier

Naive Bayes is a probabilistic ML classification algorithm built directly on Bayes' Theorem. It is called "naive" because it makes the simplifying assumption that all features are conditionally independent given the class — which is rarely true, but works well in practice.

Naive Bayes — Full Classification Formula
P(Class | x₁, x₂, ..., xₙ) ∝ P(Class) × ∏ P(xᵢ | Class)

For each class: compute this score. Pick the class with the highest score.

The "naive" assumption: P(x₁,x₂,...xₙ|Class) = P(x₁|Class) × P(x₂|Class) × ... × P(xₙ|Class)

Types of Naive Bayes

Gaussian NB: For continuous features (assumes normal distribution). Used in medical diagnosis.

Multinomial NB: For discrete counts (word counts in text). Best for document classification.

Bernoulli NB: For binary features (word present/absent). Good for short texts, spam filtering.

Strengths & Weaknesses

✅ Extremely fast to train and predict
✅ Works great with small data
✅ Handles many features (high dimensional)
✅ Probabilistic output (confidence scores)
✅ Immune to irrelevant features

❌ Strong independence assumption
❌ Poor with correlated features
❌ Zero-frequency problem (use Laplace smoothing)

📐 Worked Example — Weather & Play Cricket?

Training Data (14 days): Sunny: 9 days, Rainy: 5 days | Played cricket on 9 days, Didn't on 5 days.

P(Play=Yes) = 9/14 = 0.643    P(Play=No) = 5/14 = 0.357
P(Sunny | Play=Yes) = 6/9 = 0.667
P(Sunny | Play=No) = 3/5 = 0.60
P(Windy | Play=Yes) = 3/9 = 0.333
P(Windy | Play=No) = 3/5 = 0.60

New Day: It's Sunny AND Windy. Will we play cricket?

1

Score(Yes) = P(Yes) × P(Sunny|Yes) × P(Windy|Yes) = 0.643 × 0.667 × 0.333 = 0.143

2

Score(No) = P(No) × P(Sunny|No) × P(Windy|No) = 0.357 × 0.60 × 0.60 = 0.128

3

Normalize: P(Yes) = 0.143/(0.143+0.128) = 0.528, P(No) = 0.472

4

Prediction: Play = YES with 52.8% confidence. (It's a close call because of the wind!)

📊 Real-World Applications of Naive Bayes
  • Spam/Ham Email Filtering: Gmail uses variants of Naive Bayes. Trained on millions of emails. Each word updates P(spam | this word).
  • Sentiment Analysis: Classify product reviews as positive/negative/neutral based on word probabilities. Very fast for real-time applications.
  • Medical Diagnosis: Given symptoms (features), compute P(disease | symptoms) for multiple diseases. Fast and interpretable — doctors can understand the probabilities.
  • News Article Classification: Label news articles into categories (Sports, Tech, Politics). Multinomial NB is a strong baseline.
  • Real-Time Recommendation: When speed matters more than accuracy. Naive Bayes can classify in microseconds — critical for ad click prediction at scale.
  • Fraud Detection (initial pass): First layer filtering. Fast Naive Bayes flags suspicious transactions; heavier models (XGBoost) then do deeper analysis.
📌

Laplace Smoothing: If a word never appeared in training data, P(word|class) = 0, which zeroes out the entire product. Fix: add 1 to all counts → P(word|class) = (count + 1)/(total + vocab_size). This is called add-one (Laplace) smoothing and is standard in all Naive Bayes implementations.

📋 Section Summary — Bayes' Big Picture

Bayes' Theorem is not just a formula — it is a philosophy of reasoning under uncertainty. In classical (frequentist) statistics, probability is about long-run frequencies. In Bayesian statistics, probability represents degree of belief that gets updated as evidence accumulates.

Bayesian Thinking — Belief Update Cycle
🤖

Why Bayes' Theorem matters in AI: Modern AI systems like large language models, speech recognition, autonomous vehicles, and medical AI all use probabilistic reasoning rooted in Bayesian principles. When your phone's autocorrect suggests a word, when Netflix recommends a movie, or when a self-driving car decides whether an obstacle is real — they're all doing Bayesian-style inference: updating probability estimates based on incoming evidence.
